# Analytics

Analyze the structure, growth, completeness, and historical patterns of the main family lines. Avoid treating it as a representative population: spouses and their ancestors are intentionally under-recorded, and birth/death dates are missing unevenly.

Organize the analysis into these areas.

1. Data-quality and coverage analysis

- Number of people, families, unions, and generations
- Percentage with known birth year, death year, birthplace, residence, and parent links
- Missing dates by generation and family branch
- People with no parents, one parent, or two parents recorded
- Unconnected people and separate family components

Make a completeness heatmap by family branch and generation to reveal where research is strong and where the tree thins out.

2. Tree structure

- Number of descendants from each founding ancestor
- Number of generations documented for each main family
- Branch sizes and how they changed over time
- Cousin marriages or connections between previously separate branches
- “Bridge people” who connect major families through marriage
- Extinct branches versus branches that continue in the records
- Average number of recorded children per couple, by period and family
- Largest sibling groups
- Direct ancestors versus collateral relatives

3. Timeline and generational patterns

- Earliest and latest documented generations
- People alive during each decade
- Births, deaths, and marriages by decade
- Typical length of a generation
- Average parental age at a child’s birth
- Age differences between spouses
- Overlap between generations—for example, whether grandparents likely lived during a grandchild’s childhood

Because only a minority have dates, every chart should show both the count analyzed and the percentage of eligible people with known dates.

4. Longevity and mortality

- Age at death
- Median lifespan by birth cohort
- Lifespan by sex, family branch
- Infant and childhood mortality
- Couples’ comparative longevity
- Long-lived individuals and families

Only compare people born sufficiently long ago to have completed their lifetimes. A missing death date must not automatically mean “still living.”

5. Fertility and household formation

For couples whose children are reasonably complete:

- Number of children per couple
- Mother’s age at first and last recorded child
- Birth intervals between siblings
- Family size by generation or decade
- Proportion of adults with recorded spouses or children
- Remarriage and blended-family patterns
- Frequency of children apparently named after parents or grandparents

These results should be labeled “recorded children,” since incomplete research can resemble low fertility.

6. Surnames and naming patterns

- Frequency of surnames and surname variants
- Recurring given names within family branches
- Names passed between generations
- Patronymic or namesake patterns

Essential bias controls

Your project should distinguish among:

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Situation|Meaning|
|Unknown value|Not yet discovered|
|Not applicable|The event or relationship does not apply|
|Inferred value|Estimated from other facts|
|Recorded value|Supported by a source|
|Living/private|Intentionally suppressed|
|Minimal spouse branch|Research stopped by project design|

That last category is particularly important. Otherwise, the software may conclude that the main families have deeper ancestry, better documentation, or more interconnectedness than spouse families when that difference is simply how the project was constructed.

## Recommended dashboard

Include these 5 views:

1. Project overview: people, couples, generations, family branches, and date coverage.
2. Completeness: missing-data matrix by generation and branch.
3. Timeline: births, deaths, and people living by decade.
4. Naming patterns
5. Lifespan analysis
