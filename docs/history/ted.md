# Ted Pyrce and the Hewitt B-29 Superfortress crew over Japan

![The Lt. Hewitt B-29 crew training in Pyote, Texas 'Rattlesnake Base'.  Ted is second from left, top row.](../../media/Ted/1940s%20-%20B29%20Lost%20-%20Ted%20Top%20Row%20in%20Picture.jpg)

Ted Pyrce joined the Army Air Force in World War II and trained as the radio operator on a B-29 Superfortress crew.
He was added to the crew
commanded by 1st Lieutenant James Hewitt, Pilot and Commander in Pyote, Texas where the team completed their training
before being sent to the Pacific theatre.
He was 22 at the time.

Ted became ill with influenza just as the crew was to be deployed, and was so sick that he required hospitalization.  
Since the crew was being deployed, he was replaced by one of the radio operator trainers, T/Sg. William Andrews, who
had recently returned from serving in Europe.

When Ted recovered, he became an instructor in turn and served in that capacity until his military service was
complete.
![Ted Pyrce portrait.](../../media/Ted/1940s%20-%20Aviator.jpg)

The crew was stationed on Guam in the Mariana Islands in the Pacific, which a base for air operations against 
Japan.  They were part of the 28th Bomber Squadron, 19th Bomber Group.  The war was almost over, and Japanese air defenses
were degraded.

On July 27, 1945, B-29A 42-94098 with a crew of twelve took off for a bombing mission to Omuta, Japan.
The raid consisted of 350 B-29s.  Shortly after dropping their bombs, they were hit by flak and possibly attacked
by Japanese Zero fighters.  The crew abandoned the damaged bomber, which broke into several pieces and
crashed into a mountainous area.  Several crew members were killed in the crash or immediately after.
Although this was a rural area, all of the survivors were captured within a few days and sent to the prison camp
at Fukuoka.  They were the only plane that was lost.

Here was the crew roster:

- 1st Lt. James E. Hewitt — Pilot
- F/O Gerald E. Boleyn — Co-Pilot
- F/O Charles S. Appleby — Engineer / Navigator
- 1st Lt. John W. Gothie — Bombardier / Navigator
- 2nd Lt. Wayne A. Whitely — Radar Operator / Navigator / Bombardier
- S/Sgt. Ben Thornton — Flight Engineer / Radio Operator / Gunner
- T/Sgt. William N. Andrews — Radio Operator / Radar Observer / Gunner
- Cpl. Robert J. Zancker — Gunner
- Cpl. Martin W. O'Brien Jr. — Gunner (Central Fire Control)
- Cpl. Frederick A. Stearns — Gunner
- Cpl. Robert R. Sawdye — Tail Gunner
- Capt. Louis W. Nelson — Observer

Very shortly after the crew was captured, the US dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, and Japan surrendered.

The treatment of war prisoners by the Japanese in World War II was brutal, partly because of a cultural belief that surrendering to
an enemy was dishonorable.  US airmen in particular were considered to be war criminals because of
the bombing of civilian areas with incendiary bombs, causing extensive civilian casualties.

The surviving B-29 crew members were executed by their Japanese captors in two groups with gruesome methods
including decapitation.  One group was executed before the surrender, the second group after the surrender to help
disguise what had been done.

Charles Appleby, the Navigator, was 21 when he died.  He had married his high school sweetheart as a teenager and
they had a young daughter together.  He carried on an extensive correspondance with his wife, Marilyn, and other
friends and family members.  These were the basis of a book, **When the Letters Stopped**, by Heather Buchanan, a
descendant of Appleby.  Besides the correspondance, it
is the result of extensive research into the time, accounts
of survivors, and testimony from Japanese records and war crime trials after the war.  This book contains
a remarkably detailed account focused on Appleby and his friends and relatives, including a recreation 
of what their experiences in Japan must have been like. Ted is a minor character.
