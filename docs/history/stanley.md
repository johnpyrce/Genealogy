# Stanley Pyrce and Monte Battaglia

![Monte Battaglia Memorial](../../media/StanleyPyrce/1988%20-%20Monte%20Battaglia%20Memorial.jpg)

The Battle of [Monte Battaglia](https://www.google.com/search?kgmid=%2Fg%2F11yb55812p&q=Battle+of+Monte+Battaglia) (September 24 – October 11, 1944) was one of the bloodiest and most strategically vital engagements during the Allied attempt to pierce Nazi Germany's Gothic Line in northern Italy. Known to American soldiers as "Battle Mountain," the 715-meter peak dominated the rugged Apennine terrain. Capturing it was critical to opening the route toward Imola and breaking through into the vital Po Valley. 

## The Convergence: Partisans and 'Blue Devils'

The battle began as a coordinated effort between local resistance and conventional forces. On September 24, 1944, 250 partisans from the 36th Garibaldi Brigade began an infiltration movement, successfully occupying the medieval fortress at the summit by the morning of September 27. 

Concurrently, the U.S. Fifth Army was pushing north. The 2nd Battalion of the 350th Infantry Regiment (part of the famed 88th Infantry Division, nicknamed the "Blue Devils") pushed through nearby ridges. On the afternoon of September 27, the American troops reached the castle summit to link up with and relieve the partisan forces. 

## Seven Days of Hell

The position was immediately precarious. The 350th Infantry found themselves isolated and exposed on three sides. The surrounding German-held peaks gave Nazi artillery observers clear lines of sight. The only pipeline for Allied supplies and reinforcements was a razor-thin, mud-slicked mountain ridge heavily targeted by enemy mortar fire. 
Field Marshal Albert Kesselring threw elements of four German divisions (including the 715th and 44th Grenadiers) into a relentless counter-offensive to retake the mountain. 

- **The Crimson Crest (Sept 28)**: A massive morning assault brought waves of German infantry within yards of the summit. Fierce, close-quarters hand-to-hand combat, bayonet charges, and intense grenade duels ensued. When ammunition ran low, desperate American soldiers resorted to hurling rocks down the slopes to hold their ground. 
- **The Fog Assault (Sept 29)**: Utilizing thick autumn fog, a regimental-strength German force scaled the steep northwestern slope, briefly breaching the castle walls before a ferocious American counter-push drove them back. 
- **Flamethrowers and Shelling (Sept 30)**: German shock troops penetrated the ruins using flamethrowers. Despite the blinding fire and non-stop artillery pounding, the garrison refused to buckle. 

## Acts of Valor

The intensity of the defense at Monte Battaglia resulted in two of the 88th Division's most legendary Medals of Honor: 

- **Captain Robert E. Roeder**: On September 28, despite being rendered unconscious by shell fragments and suffering severe wounds that left him unable to stand, Roeder refused medical evacuation. He dragged himself back to his post, propped himself up against a ruined doorway, and continued to command his men and fire his weapon until he was killed. 

- **Staff Sergeant Manuel V. Mendoza**: On October 4, while wounded and facing an onslaught of roughly 200 enemy troops, Mendoza single-handedly broke the German momentum. Moving aggressively between positions, he utilized a sub-machine gun, a carbine, pistols, and an abandoned heavy machine gun to kill 30 enemy soldiers and hold the crest. 

## The British Relief and the Aftermath

Exhausted, battered, and having suffered near 50% casualties, the American defenders were systematically relieved between October 1 and October 4 by the British 1st Guards Brigade (including the Grenadier and Welsh Guards). 

The victory was absolute but came at a staggering cost. More than 2,000 Allied casualties were sustained in the immediate sector. The intense artillery duels completely pulverized what remained of the medieval fortress, leaving only the hollowed trunk of the tower standing today. In recognition of their extraordinary heroism, the 350th Infantry Regiment was awarded the Distinguished Unit Citation and proudly adopted the official regimental special designation: "Mount Battaglia".
